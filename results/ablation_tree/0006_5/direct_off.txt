## Comment Submission and Identity Information

- Regulations.gov and agency-specific comment websites collect identity information through required and optional form fields such as name, address, and email.
- Agencies can accept anonymous comments in practice by allowing fictitious or minimal entries in required fields.
- The APA does not require agencies to authenticate identity information, and the systems do not verify the validity of submitted identity data.

## Website Logs and User Tracking Data

- Regulations.gov and agency-specific websites collect event log and proxy server data such as IP address, browser type, operating system, and visit timestamps.
- This information is collected for system security and performance management, separate from the comment submission process.
- The log data cannot be reliably linked to specific comments.

## Internal Guidance and Comment Handling Practices

- Seven of the 10 selected agencies had some internal guidance related to commenter identity during the intake, analysis, and response phases.
- Guidance was most often related to intake and response activities, such as handling duplicate comments and referring to commenters in final rules.
- Agency practices for treating identity information varied across the three phases of the public comment process.

## Duplicate Comments and Public Posting Practices

- Agencies differed in how they post duplicate comments during intake, which can lead to inconsistent presentation of identity information on public websites.
- Some agencies retain all comments in the system, while others keep some duplicate records outside the system, such as in email archives.
- When agencies post only a sample of duplicate comments, the full set of identity information and unique comment text may not be visible to the public.
- SEC’s example showed a practice of posting one example per set of duplicate comments and displaying the total count received.

## Communication of Posting Policies and Data Limitations

- The agencies reviewed did not effectively communicate limitations and inconsistencies in how identity information is posted.
- Regulations.gov provides a general disclosure about identity information, but does not explain what identity data users should expect to find in comment records.
- Users are directed to agency websites for more detail, but most participating agency sites mainly direct users back to Regulations.gov or the Federal Register.
- FDA was the only participating agency noted as providing posting-practice details beyond what is available on Regulations.gov.
- EBSA recreated rulemaking materials on its own website and linked to individual comment files, but its pages did not always match Regulations.gov.

## Implications for Public Access and Rulemaking Participation

- The lack of clear disclosure about duplicate comments and identity information may lead users to misunderstand how many comments were submitted on an issue.
- Inconsistent posting practices limit users’ ability to explore and use the data and may affect meaningful participation in rulemaking.
- FCC was identified as clearly communicating its comment-posting policies and identity-information practices on its website and ECFS page.

## Follow-Up on Prior Recommendations

- In June 2019, recommendations were made for agencies to establish posting policies and more clearly communicate those policies, especially regarding identity information and duplicate comments.
- SEC completed actions responsive to the recommendation by issuing an internal memorandum and adding a public disclaimer on SEC.gov.
- BLM, CFPB, EPA, and FWS reported they were developing or updating posting policies and website statements.
- HHS stated CMS would update its policy and communicate it on the CMS website, but no finalized policy or website change was provided.
- EBSA stated it would develop a written policy on posting comments, including duplicate comments.
- WHD said it would add text to rulemaking pages stating personal information in comments will be posted to Regulations.gov without change, but the text did not clearly explain its duplicate-comment posting practice.
