## Overview

- The report focused on federal, state, and local oversight and investigation mechanisms for nursing home abuse allegations in the United States.
- It also identified gaps in CMS data, guidance, and reporting processes.

## Regulatory and Oversight Framework

- Nursing homes must comply with Medicare and Medicaid participation requirements, including residents’ rights, quality of care, and quality of life.
- CMS oversight involves central, regional, and state-level entities.
- State survey agencies conduct:
  - Routine unannounced standard inspections
  - Investigations of complaints and self-reported facility incidents
- When abuse is substantiated, reporting is required to:
  - Local law enforcement
  - Medicaid Fraud Control Units, when applicable
  - State nursing assistant registries or other licensing entities

## Abuse Deficiencies and Enforcement Trends

- Abuse deficiencies increased from 430 in 2013 to 875 in 2017, a 103.5% increase.
- In 2017, abuse deficiencies were more often cited at high severity levels, including actual harm or immediate jeopardy.
- Most abuse deficiencies were identified through complaint investigations or investigations of self-reported incidents rather than routine standard surveys.
- CMS rarely imposed remedies for abuse deficiencies; civil money penalties were the most common remedy when action was taken.

## Common Abuse Types and Perpetrators

- The most common abuse types were:
  - Physical abuse
  - Verbal/psychological abuse
  - Sexual abuse
- Perpetrators were commonly:
  - Staff
  - Residents
  - Other individuals such as family members or visitors

## Risk Factors

- Resident-related risk factors included:
  - Few visitors
  - Cognitive impairment
  - Living with residents of very different ages
- Facility-related risk factors included:
  - Staffing shortages
  - Inadequate staff training
  - Insufficient background checks

## Investigation and Reporting Challenges

- Abuse is often underreported.
- Residents with cognitive impairment may have difficulty describing what happened.
- Some facilities do not cooperate, which can delay or obscure information.
- Coordination problems among agencies can delay investigations and required reporting.

## CMS-Identified Gaps

- CMS data did not easily identify abuse type or perpetrator type.
- Facilities lacked clear guidance for completing self-reported incident documentation.
- Reporting to law enforcement and MFCUs included delays and missing reports.
- States had inconsistent interpretations of what constituted substantiated abuse.
- Rules governing information sharing with law enforcement were unclear.

## GAO Recommendations and HHS Response

- GAO made six recommendations, including:
  - Requiring states to report abuse and perpetrator types in CMS data and analyze trends
  - Issuing standardized self-report forms and guidance
  - Requiring immediate reporting to law enforcement when there is reasonable suspicion of a crime
  - Strengthening oversight to ensure timely and complete reporting
  - Clarifying that abuse can be substantiated and reported even if a penalty cannot be imposed
  - Providing guidance on what information should be included when reporting to law enforcement
- HHS agreed with all six recommendations and stated it would:
  - Review data fields
  - Develop standardized forms
  - Strengthen reporting and follow-up
  - Clarify definitions and information-sharing rules

## Action Items

- CMS / HHS: Review and add data fields to identify abuse type and perpetrator type
- CMS / HHS: Develop and publish standardized self-report forms and guidance for facility incident reporting
- CMS / HHS: Require immediate reporting to law enforcement when there is reasonable suspicion of a crime
- CMS / HHS: Strengthen oversight to ensure reporting is timely and complete
- CMS / HHS: Clarify substantiated abuse definitions and information-sharing rules
- CMS / HHS: Provide guidance on what information should be included in reports to law enforcement
