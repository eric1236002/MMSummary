## Oversight and Investigation Structure

- Nursing home oversight is shared among CMS central office, CMS regional offices, and state survey agencies.
- CMS central office oversees federal nursing home standards and issues guidance through the State Operations Manual.
- CMS regional offices oversee state activities and evaluate state surveyor performance.
- State survey agencies conduct unannounced standard surveys and investigate complaints and facility-reported incidents.
- Nursing homes must report alleged abuse immediately to the state survey agency, conduct their own investigation, and submit a written report within 5 working days.
- Substantiated abuse must be referred to local law enforcement, the Medicaid Fraud Control Unit if appropriate, and the nurse aide registry or other relevant licensing authority.

## Enforcement Actions

- CMS may impose enforcement actions when deficiencies are cited, including directed in-service training, civil money penalties, denial of payment, and termination from Medicare and Medicaid participation.
- Some penalties are immediate, while others may be avoided if the nursing home corrects the issue before the effective date.
- Mandatory termination for abuse deficiencies was extremely rare during the review period.

## Abuse Investigation Challenges and Coordination

- Other entities involved in abuse investigations may include Adult Protective Services, long-term care ombudsmen, local law enforcement, and Medicaid Fraud Control Units.
- Stakeholders reported delays in investigations when nursing homes delay access to records or alter records after incidents.
- Cooperation from nursing homes was described as inconsistent and sometimes dependent on the seriousness of the allegation.
- Multiple agencies involved in investigations can create coordination challenges.
- Some stakeholders said CMS restrictions limit what information state survey agencies can share with law enforcement.

## CMS Data Limitations and Reporting Gaps

- CMS does not readily access data on abuse type or perpetrator type in its datasets.
- In two CMS datasets, state survey agencies are not required to record abuse type and perpetrator type.
- CMS officials stated their understanding of abuse patterns is based on professional experience, literature, and ad hoc analysis rather than systematic collection of abuse-type data.
- Facility-reported incident information was often described by state survey agencies as incomplete, requiring follow-up and delaying investigations.
- CMS has not provided guidance on what information must be included in facility-reported incident reports.

## Trends in Abuse-Related Deficiencies

- Abuse deficiencies increased from 430 in 2013 to 875 in 2017.
- Abuse deficiencies remained less than 1% of all deficiencies, but became more frequent over time.
- The share of abuse deficiencies causing actual harm or immediate jeopardy increased from 31.9% in 2013 to 42.6% in 2017.
- Abuse-related deficiencies increased from 4,899 in 2013 to 5,383 in 2017, with a peak in 2016 at 5,687.
- More than half of abuse-related deficiencies each year were identified through standard surveys.
- The proportion identified through complaint investigations and facility-reported incident investigations increased over time.

## Nursing Home Patterns

- The number of nursing homes with at least one abuse deficiency more than doubled from 394 in 2013 to 821 in 2017.
- 2,214 unique nursing homes had at least one abuse deficiency over the five-year period.
- Some nursing homes had abuse deficiencies in multiple consecutive years, suggesting recurring issues.
- For-profit nursing homes and Special Focus Facilities were more represented among homes with repeated abuse deficiencies.

## Types of Abuse and Perpetrators

- In sampled CMS narratives from 2016 and 2017, physical abuse and mental/verbal abuse were the most common types, followed by sexual abuse.
- Staff were the most common perpetrators, followed by residents, then other individuals such as family members or visitors.
- Mental/verbal abuse was less often categorized as severe than physical or sexual abuse.
- Most narratives were categorized as isolated incidents in scope.

## Risk Factors for Abuse

- Resident-related risk factors included lack of frequent visitors, cognitive impairment, and mixing residents with widely different age groups.
- Staffing-related risk factors included insufficient staffing, inadequate staff training on abuse, and inadequate staff screening.

## Recommendations and HHS Response

- The report included six recommendations to CMS on data collection, guidance, referral requirements, oversight, substantiation guidance, and law enforcement referral content.
- HHS concurred with all six recommendations.
- HHS stated it would explore options to require abuse and perpetrator type data, develop standardized guidance for facility-reported incidents, require immediate referrals when reasonable suspicion of a crime exists, consider mechanisms for tracking referrals, clarify substantiation guidance, and develop standardized referral elements.

## Action Items

- **CMS/HHS**: Evaluate options to require state survey agencies to record abuse type and perpetrator type in CMS databases.
- **CMS/HHS**: Develop and issue standardized guidance and a template for facility-reported incident information.
- **CMS/HHS**: Update referral requirements so state survey agencies immediately refer qualifying complaints and surveys to law enforcement and MFCUs when applicable.
- **CMS/HHS**: Oversee state survey agencies to ensure timely referrals of complaints, surveys, and substantiated incidents.
- **CMS/HHS**: Clarify guidance on when allegations verified by evidence should be substantiated and reported even if citing a federal deficiency is not appropriate.
- **CMS/HHS**: Provide guidance on what information should be included in referrals of abuse allegations to law enforcement.
