## Cybersecurity Risks and Third-Party Tax Data Protection

- Federal agencies, including IRS, rely on computerized systems and electronic data to carry out operations and protect sensitive taxpayer information.
- Third-party providers such as paid preparers and tax software providers retain large amounts of taxpayer data and are targets for data theft and identity theft refund fraud.
- Identity theft tax refund fraud involves stealing taxpayer data and then using it to file fraudulent returns and claim refunds.

## IRS Oversight Structure for Third-Party Providers

- Multiple IRS offices have responsibilities related to third-party provider security oversight, including Stakeholder Liaison, Cybersecurity, Criminal Investigation, Return Preparer Office, Small Business/Self-Employed Examination, Electronic Products and Services Support, Return Integrity and Compliance Services, and Office of Professional Responsibility.
- The Office of Professional Responsibility administers practitioner regulations but does not oversee third-party information security.
- IRS coordinates some activities across offices, but there is no centralized leadership or formal governance structure for protecting taxpayer information held by third-party providers.

## Existing Security Requirements and Gaps

- IRS uses the Authorized e-file Provider program to prescribe requirements for certain third-party providers.
- IRS Revenue Procedure 2007-40 states that compliance with the FTC Safeguards Rule is required for participation in the program.
- IRS publications reference the FTC Safeguards Rule but do not fully explain the required elements of a written information security program.
- IRS has specific security standards for online providers, but not for all provider types.
- IRS does not have a robust set of minimum information security standards for all tax software providers or paid preparers.
- IRS has not fully incorporated the NIST Special Publication 800-53 controls agreed to by Security Summit participants into requirements for all tax software providers.
- The security standards for online providers in Publication 1345 have not been substantially updated since 2010.
- One referenced encryption minimum, TLS 1.0, is outdated compared with NIST and industry guidance.

## Outreach and Education Efforts

- IRS uses publications, webinars, forums, social media, news releases, and campaigns to educate third-party providers about security risks and leading practices.
- Outreach examples included the Tax Security 101 campaign, tax forums, webinars, YouTube videos, and Twitter posts.
- IRS and industry groups noted that outreach is harder to reach small paid preparers who are not connected to larger industry channels.

## Monitoring and Compliance Oversight

- IRS monitoring is focused primarily on Electronic Return Originators (EROs) and physical information security.
- Monitoring guidance and job aids provide limited direction on cybersecurity or electronic information security controls.
- IRS officials stated that revenue agents do not have the technical expertise to fully assess cybersecurity controls.
- Monitoring for online providers is limited and not systematic across all six online-provider standards.
- IRS has not recently assessed whether different monitoring approaches are needed for different provider types.
- IRS’s monitoring approach has not been substantially updated since 2011.

## Security Incident Reporting and Data Use

- Security incident reporting requirements apply differently across provider types.
- Online providers must report incidents promptly; paid preparers and EROs are not subject to the same standardized reporting requirement.
- IRS uses incident information for fraud detection, trend analysis, account monitoring, and criminal investigations.
- IRS tracks incidents in the RICS Incident Management Database, but does not have a comprehensive document describing intake, storage, and sharing of incident data across IRS offices.
- Some reporting and sharing mechanisms are voluntary, including Security Summit and ISAC channels.

## GAO Conclusions and Recommendations

- GAO concluded that IRS’s third-party provider security requirements have not kept pace with changing cyber risks.
- GAO noted missed opportunities due to lack of centralized leadership, outdated standards, inconsistent monitoring, and inconsistent incident reporting.
- GAO stated that Congress should consider providing IRS explicit authority to establish security requirements for the systems of paid preparers and Authorized e-file Providers.
- IRS agreed with three recommendations and disagreed with five.

## Action Items

- Congress: Consider providing IRS explicit authority to establish security requirements for the information systems of paid preparers and Authorized e-file Providers.
- Commissioner of Internal Revenue: Develop a governance structure or centralized leadership to coordinate taxpayer information protection efforts at third-party providers.
- Commissioner of Internal Revenue: Modify Authorized e-file Provider requirements to explicitly state the FTC Safeguards Rule information security program elements.
- Commissioner of Internal Revenue: Require all tax software providers in the Authorized e-file Provider program to follow the agreed subset of NIST Special Publication 800-53 controls.
- Commissioner of Internal Revenue: Regularly review and update security requirements for tax software providers and other Authorized e-file Providers.
- Commissioner of Internal Revenue: Update ERO monitoring programs to include basic information security and cybersecurity techniques, and revise guidance, job aids, and training as needed.
- Commissioner of Internal Revenue: Conduct a risk assessment of monitoring approaches for all Authorized e-file Provider types and revise monitoring accordingly if needed.
- Commissioner of Internal Revenue: Standardize incident reporting requirements for all Authorized e-file Providers.
- Commissioner of Internal Revenue: Document the intake, storage, and sharing of security incident data across IRS offices.
