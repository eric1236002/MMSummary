## Regulatory Oversight Framework

- FDA generally oversees most domestic and imported foods, while USDA oversees meat, poultry, processed egg products, and catfish.
- FDA uses a risk-based inspection approach for food facilities.
- USDA inspection requirements vary by facility type, including at least once per operating shift or continuous presence.
- FDA regulates food additives and GRAS substances under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.
- USDA requires preapproval of labels for products under its jurisdiction, while FDA focuses on preventing misbranding after products enter the market.

## Cell-Cultured Meat Production and Technical Uncertainty

- The cell-cultured meat production process was described in five phases: biopsy, cell banking, growth, harvest, and food processing.
- Commercial-scale production details remain unclear, including growth medium composition, use of genetic engineering, antibiotics, scaffolds, harvest definition, and final product composition.
- Many firms are protecting proprietary information, limiting what regulators and stakeholders know about current and future production methods.
- The technology is still in development, and commercial production methods are not fully established.

## Commercialization and Market Challenges

- Open questions remain about biopsy frequency, source animals, use of genetic engineering, antibiotic residues, growth media, scaffold type, scale-up, production costs, profitability, safety, market timing, and consumer acceptance.
- It is unclear whether products can be sold at a profitable and affordable price.
- It is not yet known whether cell-cultured meat will pose safety hazards different from conventional meat.
- Potential environmental, animal welfare, and human health benefits have been discussed, but these have not been fully substantiated.

## FDA-USDA Coordination and Interagency Agreement

- In 2018, FDA and USDA held a joint public meeting, received public comments, and began developing a shared oversight approach.
- In March 2019, the agencies issued an interagency agreement assigning FDA oversight through the point of harvest and USDA oversight after harvest through food processing and labeling for cell-cultured meat from USDA-amenable species.
- In June 2019, the agencies created three working groups: pre-market assessment, labeling, and transfer of jurisdiction.
- The interagency agreement is intended to be reviewed every three years.

## Collaboration Gaps and Oversight Concerns

- The interagency agreement and working groups only partially incorporate leading practices for interagency collaboration.
- Gaps were noted in defining outcomes, tracking progress, leadership continuity, documentation of collaboration procedures, and inclusion of all relevant participants.
- The agencies had not formally documented oversight responsibility for cell-cultured seafood other than catfish.
- Stakeholders raised concerns about potential fragmentation, overlap, and unclear oversight boundaries, especially at the harvest transfer point.

## Labeling and Consumer Communication

- Labeling was identified as a major issue for both conventional and cell-cultured meat stakeholders.
- USDA has committed to a public process, likely rulemaking, to develop labeling requirements for cell-cultured meat and poultry.
- The United States Cattlemen’s Association petitioned USDA in February 2018 to restrict the use of the terms “beef” and “meat” to traditionally produced animal products.
- Several states have enacted labeling laws affecting plant-based and cell-cultured products.
- Consumer acceptance remains uncertain and is considered a major commercialization challenge.

## GAO Report Findings and Agency Responses

- The GAO report found that FDA and USDA partially incorporated all seven leading practices for interagency collaboration.
- The report recommended improved documentation, clearer accountability, and formal documentation of seafood oversight.
- FDA and USDA partially or fully concurred with the recommendations.
- Both agencies stated they preferred to incorporate collaboration practices into a more detailed framework or standard operating procedure rather than revising the interagency agreement immediately.
