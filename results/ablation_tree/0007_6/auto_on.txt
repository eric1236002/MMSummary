## Medicare Hospice Benefit and Covered Services

- Medicare hospice eligibility requires enrollment in Medicare Part A and certification as terminally ill with a life expectancy of 6 months or less if the illness runs its normal course.
- Beneficiaries must elect the hospice benefit and waive Medicare payment for curative treatment related to the terminal illness.
- Hospice care may be provided at home or in settings such as nursing homes, assisted living facilities, hospice facilities, and hospitals.
- Covered services include physician and nursing care, medical equipment and supplies, drugs for pain and symptom management, hospice aide and homemaker services, therapy, and spiritual/grief counseling.
- Hospice interdisciplinary teams develop care plans with beneficiaries, families, and caregivers.
- Hospice services are available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.
- Beneficiaries may remain in hospice beyond 6 months if eligibility continues, and may disenroll and re-enroll later.

## Hospice Payment Structure

- CMS pays hospices based on the level of care provided each day.
- There are four hospice care levels, paid through daily or hourly rates depending on location and intensity.
- Payment rates are adjusted geographically and updated annually.
- Routine home care is the most common level of care and is paid daily regardless of whether services are delivered on that day.
- CMS applies payment caps for inpatient days and total annual Medicare payments.

## Quality Reporting and Survey Oversight

- CMS’s Hospice Quality Reporting Program includes provider-reported data through the Hospice Item Set and caregiver experience data through the CAHPS Hospice Survey.
- Quality results are published on Hospice Compare.
- CMS oversees hospice compliance through surveys conducted by state survey agencies or CMS-approved accrediting organizations.
- Surveys may be initial certification surveys, recertification surveys, or complaint/incident surveys.
- Deficiencies are classified as:
  - Condition-level deficiencies, which are the most serious and can trigger a 90-day termination track
  - Standard-level deficiencies, which require an acceptable plan of correction

## For-Profit and Non-Profit Hospice Provider Comparisons

- In 2017, for-profit hospices served about 50 percent of Medicare hospice beneficiaries, and non-profit hospices served about 48 percent.
- For-profit hospices represented about two-thirds of all hospice providers.
- Beneficiary demographics were generally similar across provider types, with two main differences:
  - Non-profit hospices had slightly higher percentages of white beneficiaries
  - For-profit hospices had a greater share of dual Medicare-Medicaid beneficiaries
- For-profit hospices served a higher proportion of beneficiaries with non-cancer diagnoses:
  - 77 percent for-profit
  - 69 percent non-profit

## Medicare Hospice Payments by Provider Type

- For-profit providers received a larger share of Medicare hospice payments than non-profit providers.
- In 2017:
  - For-profit providers received about $10.4 billion, or 58 percent of total payments
  - Non-profit providers received about $7.2 billion, or 40 percent of total payments
- This pattern was consistent from 2014 through 2017.
- Longer average lengths of stay for for-profit hospices, especially for non-cancer beneficiaries, were identified as a contributing factor.
- For-profit providers had longer average lengths of stay for both cancer and non-cancer beneficiaries than non-profit providers.

## Quality Measure and Caregiver Experience Findings

- Average scores on CMS quality measures were generally similar for for-profit and non-profit hospices.
- For the seven provider-reported quality measures, performance was high for both groups.
- On the composite measure implemented in 2017:
  - For-profit hospices averaged 83.6 percent
  - Non-profit hospices averaged 87.0 percent
- For-profit hospices were more often among the lowest-performing providers:
  - 12 percent of for-profit providers were in the 10th percentile or lower
  - 6 percent of non-profit providers were in the 10th percentile or lower
- 130 providers had composite scores below 50 percent and treated more than 24,000 beneficiaries.
- Caregiver survey measures covered respectful treatment, emotional and religious support, pain and symptom help, caregiver training, caregiver ratings, and caregiver recommendation of the hospice.
- The data analysis included 2,832 hospice providers with caregiver survey data.

## Live Discharges and End-of-Life Visits

- Live discharge rates were higher for for-profit hospices than for non-profit hospices.
- In 2017:
  - 22.1 percent of beneficiaries served by for-profit hospices were discharged alive
  - 12.0 percent of beneficiaries served by non-profit hospices were discharged alive
- 472 hospice providers had live discharge rates of 50 percent or more, and nearly all were for-profit providers.
- Average total provider visits in the last 7 days of life were similar for both provider types, at about 6 visits each in 2017.
- Differences were noted in specific visit types near the end of life:
  - 77 percent of for-profit beneficiaries and 85 percent of non-profit beneficiaries received at least one visit from registered nurses, physicians, or nurse practitioners in the last 3 days of life
  - 68 percent of for-profit beneficiaries and 57 percent of non-profit beneficiaries received at least two visits from social workers, chaplains/spiritual counselors, licensed practical nurses, or hospice aides in the last 7 days of life
- Some providers provided no visits during the final days of life.

## CMS Oversight Limitations and Policy Discussion

- CMS surveyors review licensure records, prior survey findings, complaints, and other public information before surveying hospices.
- CMS does not instruct surveyors to review hospice quality measures or other risk indicators as part of survey preparation.
- CMS’s main enforcement remedy for hospice noncompliance is termination of the Medicare provider agreement.
- From 2014 through 2017, 19 hospices were involuntarily terminated.
- The transcript stated that CMS lacks additional enforcement tools available to other provider types, such as civil money penalties, denial of payment for new admissions, and directed in-service training.
- The discussion noted that hospice growth since 2000 has increased the need for stronger oversight.
- HHS concurred with the recommendation to use additional data in the survey process and stated it would look into ways to incorporate such data into hospice oversight.
- HHS also noted existing oversight requirements, including survey standards and a data-driven quality assessment and performance improvement program.

## Action Items

- **CMS Administrator**: Incorporate additional information, such as quality measures or other data that can identify potential quality-of-care issues, into the hospice survey process.
- **Congress**: Consider granting CMS authority to establish additional enforcement remedies for hospices that do not meet federal health and safety requirements.
- **HHS/CMS**: Review ways to incorporate meaningful quality measure data into hospice oversight, including survey processes.
